# Ethics, authorship and disclosure — the dense version

This is the reference beside `research-ethics`. It holds what a research run has to
settle about people — participants, authors, funders, data subjects — and it exists
because those decisions are the ones most often made in the last hour before a
submission, from memory, in sentences nobody can check.

**Read this first: everything here is about what to COLLECT and what to STATE. It
is not a determination, and it is not legal advice.** Whether a study needs
approval, what a jurisdiction's data-protection regime permits, and who may be an
author are questions for an institution, a committee and a data-protection officer.
A plugin has no jurisdiction, no committee and no standing to answer them; what it
can do is make sure the record is complete enough for someone who does.

## 1. Who decides what

The usual shape of research-ethics review, stated as a fact about committees rather
than as advice:

| the committee's own categories | typically used for | who may decide |
|---|---|---|
| exempt / not human-subjects research | some educational or secondary-data work | the committee, or a designated officer — and it is THE COMMITTEE that decides, not the researcher |
| expedited / light-touch review | minimal-risk studies | a sub-committee or a chair |
| full board review | more than minimal risk, vulnerable populations, deception, sensitive topics | the convened committee |
| institutional approval for animal work | all vertebrate research in most jurisdictions | the animal-welfare body |

Three things follow, and they are what to tell a user:

1. **Collect, do not classify.** The facts a committee asks for are: who the
   participants are, what they will do, what risks follow, how consent will be
   taken, where the data will live, and who can reach it. Those can be gathered
   without deciding anything.
2. **Record the outcome with its address.** "Approved, reference UREC-2026-0142, by
   the University Research Ethics Committee, on 12 March 2026" is a sentence a
   reader can check. "Ethics approval was obtained" is not, and a record that says
   it is the record a reviewer will query.
3. **A change to the study is usually a change to the approval.** New outcome, new
   population, new data source, longer retention: most committees require an
   amendment, and the approval reference stays the same while the version moves.

## 2. Consent

- **The form follows the study, not the template.** Written consent is the default
  for anything with signatures, specimens or identifiable data; verbal consent is
  defensible where literacy, culture or risk makes writing it down a harm — and it
  is recorded (who took it, when, and what was said) rather than assumed.
- **What an information sheet has to say**: the purpose, what participation
  involves, the risks and the benefits, that participation is voluntary, that
  withdrawal is possible and by when (and what happens to data already collected),
  who will see the data, how long it will be kept, how it will be reported, and who
  to contact — including an independent contact for complaints.
- **Capacity and assent**: children and adults without capacity usually give
  assent alongside a guardian's consent. Assent is not a signature on a form for a
  child; it is an explanation the participant can understand, and the record says
  how it was obtained.
- **Deception** is used where telling the participant the hypothesis would change
  their behaviour. It requires a justification, minimisation, and an explicit
  **debriefing** — and in many jurisdictions, a waiver of the right to withdraw.
- **Incentives** must be proportionate: payment that is large relative to a
  participant's circumstances is a pressure, not a thank-you, and it is the
  committee, not the researcher, that judges proportionality.
- **Waiving consent** happens (some secondary-data and emergency research) and it
  is a documented decision with a basis — which is why this package refuses a
  `waived` consent with no reason. A waiver with no basis is a gap a reader sees.
- **Withdrawal** has a deadline, and saying so in advance is kinder than
  explaining it afterwards: once analysis has begun or results are pooled,
  withdrawal is often impossible in practice. Say when.

## 3. Participants whose position changes the requirements

Not a list of prohibited populations — a list of situations where the record needs
more, and where a committee will ask why:

- **Children and young people**: parental consent plus assent; safeguarding duties;
  what happens if a disclosure of harm emerges during the study.
- **People in a dependent relationship** with the researcher — students, patients,
  employees, prisoners. The dependency is the risk: consent given to somebody with
  power over you is consent under pressure, and the usual answer is an independent
  recruiter, an advocate present, or a different population.
- **Cognitive impairment, acute illness, distress**: capacity can fluctuate; consent
  is a process rather than an event.
- **People who are undocumented, criminalised, or otherwise exposing themselves by
  participating**: data minimisation is not a formality here, and the record should
  say what could identify them and what was done about it.
- **Indigenous and community-based research**: community consent and data
  sovereignty principles (OCAP is the usual reference in Canada) can govern the
  data independently of individual consent. Ask who speaks for the community, and
  record the answer.
- **Participants recruited through a platform or an employer**: who holds the list,
  what the platform learns, and whether the employer can infer participation.

**Exclusion needs a reason too.** Excluding pregnant people, older adults or
non-native speakers is a decision with consequences for who the finding applies to,
and "we excluded them for their own protection" is a sentence that has been used to
keep whole populations out of the evidence base.

## 4. Data protection, in the terms a data-management plan uses

This is a description of the concepts, not a legal opinion; the applicable law is a
question for the institution's data-protection officer.

- **The lawful basis** for processing personal data, and for the special
  categories within it (health, ethnicity, sexuality, biometrics, and in some
  jurisdictions criminal records). Research exemptions exist in several regimes and
  they come with conditions — safeguards, a public-interest test, technical
  measures — that have to be met, not merely cited.
- **Minimisation**: collect what the question needs. A date of birth where a year
  will do is a fact you now have to protect.
- **Pseudonymisation is not anonymisation.** Replacing a name with a code while
  keeping the key is pseudonymised data — still personal data, still in scope. Only
  irreversibly de-identified data leaves the regime, and whether it is truly
  irreversible is easier to assume than to demonstrate.
- **Retention and destruction**: how long, decided in advance, and how it will
  actually be destroyed. "Kept indefinitely in case it is useful" is the answer that
  fails a review.
- **Access and transfer**: who inside the team can reach the data, whether it leaves
  the institution or the jurisdiction, and on what basis. A shared drive in another
  country is a transfer.
- **Participants' rights**, in regimes that grant them: access, rectification,
  erasure, restriction, objection, portability. Research can narrow some of these,
  and the narrowing has to be explained in the information sheet.
- **A data-management plan** is worth writing even where one is not required: what
  will be created, in what formats, where it lives during the project, where it goes
  afterwards, who owns it, and what happens when the project ends.

## 5. Secondary data, repositories and reuse

- **The original consent governs the new use.** Consent for a trial of one
  intervention is not consent for a pooled analysis of a different question; broad
  consent exists, and what it covers is written in the form you have, not in your
  intention.
- **The data holder's terms** are a second gate: licence, embargo, an agreement
  about who may publish first, and a requirement to not attempt re-identification.
- **An ethics exemption for the original study is not one for yours.** A secondary
  analysis can need its own review, and the honest first step is to ask the
  committee — with the original consent form in hand.
- **Repositories and accession numbers** belong in the record: a dataset with an
  accession number in the disclosure is a dataset a reader can find.

## 6. Animal research

- **The 3Rs**: replacement (can this be answered without animals?), reduction (the
  smallest number that answers it, justified by a power calculation rather than a
  habit), refinement (less pain, better housing, better handling).
- **Approval** comes from the institution's animal-welfare body, with a protocol
  number, and it is recorded like any other.
- **ARRIVE** is the reporting guideline, and it asks for what a reader needs to
  judge the work: species, strain, sex, numbers, housing, randomisation, blinding,
  humane endpoints, and how the analysis handled them.
- **Sex and strain reporting** is part of reduction in practice: a study that
  cannot say which animals it used cannot be pooled with anything.

## 7. Dual use and the risk of harm

Findings can be misused — a vulnerability, a synthesis route, a method of evasion.
The judgement is not "is this dangerous", which nobody can answer at the bench; it
is whether the work could be directly misused, whether the risk is plausible rather
than theoretical, and who should be consulted before publication. Several journals
have a process for this, and the record says what was considered.

## 8. Conflicts of interest

- **Financial**: employment, consultancy, honoraria, patents, stock, travel funded
  by an interested party, a grant from a body that benefits from the result.
- **Non-financial** and equally disclosable: a personal relationship with an author
  whose work is being evaluated, a leadership role in an organisation the finding
  concerns, a strong prior public position, an editorial role at the venue.
- **Who declares**: every author, individually, even when it feels irrelevant. The
  reader decides relevance; the author declares.
- **The form**: "X has received speaking fees from Y; the other authors declare no
  competing interests" is a disclosure. "The authors declare no conflicts of
  interest" is a sentence that is either true or isn't, and a reader has no way to
  tell which.
- **A funder's role** is part of it: what the funder did and did not have a part in
  (design, analysis, the decision to publish) is a statement only the authors can
  make, and it is the one a reader looks for.

## 9. Authorship

- **ICMJE's four criteria**, all required: substantial contribution to conception
  or design, or to acquisition, analysis or interpretation; drafting the work or
  revising it critically; final approval of the version to be published; and
  agreement to be accountable for all of it.
- **Who is therefore NOT an author**: somebody who only provided funding, only
  supplied materials, only gave general supervision, or only read a draft. Those
  are acknowledged — with permission, because a reader will assume an acknowledged
  person endorses the work.
- **CRediT's fourteen roles** are the contribution vocabulary, and they are not a
  ranking: formal analysis and funding acquisition are different jobs, not a
  ladder. The matrix in the disclosure record is what a reader checks when a
  contribution statement and an author list disagree.
- **Order** is a convention. Alphabetical, first-author-last-author, corresponding
  author as senior: it differs by field, by venue and by country, and the only
  defensible rule is to follow the field's convention and to write down anything
  unusual. A note that "the first two authors contributed equally" prevents a
  dispute that a reader would otherwise invent.
- **Guest, gift and ghost authorship** are the failure modes: somebody who did not
  contribute is listed, or somebody who did is omitted, or a writer is paid and
  hidden. All three are research misconduct in most codes, and all three are visible
  from a contribution matrix that does not add up.
- **Changes after submission** — adding, removing or reordering authors — almost
  always needs the editor's agreement and, for a removal, the removed author's. Say
  what changed and why, in the record, at the time.
- **An AI system cannot be an author**: it cannot consent, cannot take
  responsibility, and cannot be accountable. Every policy surveyed says so. Its use
  is disclosed in the methods or the acknowledgements, per the venue.

## 10. Funding

- **Every source**, with the grant number, and in the form the venue asks for: the
  funder's exact registered name, not the abbreviation the authors use.
- **The role statement**, when the venue asks for one, is where the honest sentence
  goes: which of design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, writing and the
  decision to submit the funder had no part in.
- **No funding** is a statement worth making explicitly — which is why this
  package's record distinguishes an empty list from an absent field.
- **In-kind support** — a company's data, a government's access, a hospital's staff
  time — is a funding source in substance even when no money moved.

## 11. Data and code availability

- **The categories a reader understands**: openly available (with a repository and
  a persistent identifier), available on request (with an address and, ideally, the
  terms), restricted (with a reason: consent, law, a third party's rights), or
  destroyed/not applicable.
- **"Available on request" is read as "not available"** unless it names who to ask
  and under what conditions. That is not cynicism: it is what happens when the
  request arrives after a postdoc has left.
- **Code is data** for this purpose. An analysis that cannot be run cannot be
  checked, and a script with no environment specification is a script that ran once.
- **De-identification before deposit**, and a check that the plan for it survives
  contact with the actual file: free-text fields, timestamps, small cells and
  unusual combinations are where re-identification lives.
- **A licence** on the deposited data and code, chosen deliberately: a repository's
  default may permit uses you would not have chosen.

## 12. Generative AI, as a disclosure rather than a policy debate

- **Say which tool, for what, and in what version** if the venue asks. A statement
  that names the tool and the scope is checkable; "AI was used" is not.
- **What must not go into a tool**: confidential or unpublished data belonging to
  somebody else, identifiable participant data, anything under a non-disclosure
  agreement, and material whose licence forbids it. A prompt is a disclosure to a
  third party, whatever the terms of service say about training.
- **Authorship**: the tool cannot be an author, and "assisted by AI" does not
  reduce the authors' responsibility for every sentence, reference and number.
  Verification is not delegable — a fabricated citation produced by a tool is the
  author's fabricated citation.
- **Where it goes**: an increasing number of venues specify a section (methods,
  acknowledgements, a disclosure statement) and some require it in the cover letter.
  Check the venue's policy rather than reusing another journal's sentence.
- **A useful statement has four parts**: which tool, what it was used for, what it
  was not used for, and who verified the result.

## 13. Integrity failures a statement exists to make visible

- **Fabrication** — data or results that do not exist. **Falsification** —
  manipulating, selecting or omitting data so the record misrepresents what
  happened. Both are the failures the claim ledger is built to make visible, and
  neither is caught by a rung: the ladder asks whether the words are in the source,
  never whether the experiment happened.
- **Plagiarism**, including self-plagiarism: reusing your own text without
  attribution is a copyright and disclosure problem, not a moral one.
- **Duplicate submission** and **salami slicing**: one study reported as four, each
  claiming novelty. The disclosure record is where the prior reports belong.
- **Citation manipulation**: citations added to please an editor or a reviewer,
  citations to work that does not support the sentence — which is what the r2 locate
  rung exists to catch — and coordinated citation exchanges.
- **Image and figure manipulation**: selective cropping, duplicated panels,
  adjusted brightness that changes the interpretation. Report the adjustment or do
  not make it.
- **What to do when you find one** — in your own work or a collaborator's: stop,
  do not delete anything, tell the person who can act, and expect a correction
  rather than a retraction where the record can be repaired. The instinct to fix it
  quietly is the one that turns a correction into misconduct.

## 14. Before the submission leaves the run

- [ ] every author has a CRediT role, and every role in the matrix is one somebody
      actually did;
- [ ] the author list follows the field's convention, and any equal contribution or
      unusual order is stated;
- [ ] ethics: the approving body and the reference number are recorded, or the
      reason none was needed is;
- [ ] consent: the form, the process, and any waiver with its basis;
- [ ] data protection: what is held, where, for how long, who can reach it, and
      what happens at the end;
- [ ] funding: every source with its grant number, or an explicit statement that
      there is none, plus the funder's role where the venue asks;
- [ ] conflicts: declared per author, or `none` — and the reader decides relevance;
- [ ] data and code: where they are, under what terms, or why they cannot be shared;
- [ ] AI use: the tool, the scope, what it was not used for, and who verified it;
- [ ] the title page and the disclosure are uploaded **separately** from the
      manuscript, and the manuscript carries neither;
- [ ] and the sentence that belongs in the cover letter: every statement was
      recorded by the authors, and the builder checked that each one is complete —
      not that it is true.
